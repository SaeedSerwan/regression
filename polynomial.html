Supervised learning · Regression · Lesson 2

When a straight line isn’t enough.

Stopping distance grows with the square of speed. Crop yield rises and then falls as fertiliser increases. Electricity demand follows the clock. Polynomial regression captures curved relationships like these with the same least-squares method you already know. Fitting the curve is the easy part; the real skill is choosing how flexible it should be.

1When lines fail

A straight line assumes that each extra unit of \(x\) changes \(y\) by the same amount, everywhere. Many real relationships do not behave like that. Two warning signs tell you a line is the wrong shape:

The scatter bends. The points curve away from any line you could draw.
The residuals have a pattern. Plot \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\) against \(x\). A good model leaves random scatter around 0; a U-shape or wave means structure the model missed.

Polynomial regression adds powers of \(x\) to the model:

$$\hat y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \dots + \beta_d x^d$$
\(d\) the degree: the highest power used. \(d = 1\) is the straight line from lesson 1.
\(\beta_0, \dots, \beta_d\) \(d + 1\) coefficients, all learned by least squares.
\(d = 2\) quadratic: one bend (a parabola).
\(d = 3\) cubic: up to two bends (an S-shape).

2Anatomy of a polynomial

Before fitting data, build intuition for what each coefficient does. Move the sliders, or start from a preset.

Coefficients

The leading coefficient (the highest power with a non-zero coefficient) decides what happens far from the centre: a positive β₂ opens upwards, a negative β₂ downwards. A degree-\(d\) polynomial can bend at most \(d - 1\) times. That flexibility is both its strength and its danger.

4Why it is still linear regression

The curve bends, but the model is linear in its coefficients. Each \(\beta_j\) simply multiplies a column of numbers. We create new features \(x^2, x^3, \dots\) from \(x\) and then run ordinary multiple linear regression on them. This trick is called feature engineering, or a basis expansion.

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \cdots & x_1^d \\ 1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & \cdots & x_2^d \\ \vdots & & & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \cdots & x_n^d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}$$

Look at the last row: the columns live on wildly different scales (\(x^{d}\) can be billions of times larger than \(x\)). That makes \(X^\top X\) numerically fragile. In practice, scale the features first (for example with StandardScaler). This site maps \(x\) to the range \([-1, 1]\) internally and solves with a QR decomposition for stability.

5Choosing the degree

A higher degree always lowers training error, because a more flexible curve can always pass closer to the points. So training error cannot tell you when to stop. Two better tools:

Adjusted R² penalises each extra coefficient, so it only rises if a new term earns its place: $$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1-R^2)\,\frac{n-1}{n-d-1}$$
k-fold cross-validation splits the data into \(k\) folds, trains on \(k-1\) and tests on the one left out, \(k\) times. The pooled test error estimates performance on unseen data.

This is the bias–variance trade-off. Too low a degree gives high bias: the model is too rigid and misses the shape (underfitting). Too high a degree gives high variance: the curve chases noise and changes wildly with each new sample (overfitting). Pick the degree with the lowest CV error. If two degrees are close, prefer the simpler model.

6Beware extrapolation

Inside the range of the training data a polynomial can fit beautifully. Outside it, the highest power takes over and the curve shoots towards ±∞. The shaded band is where the model has seen data; everything else is a guess.

Fits of different degrees often agree inside the data and disagree completely outside it. Never trust a polynomial’s prediction far beyond the observed \(x\) range. If you must extrapolate, use a model whose shape is justified by theory, such as the physics behind the stopping-distance or projectile data.

7Taming wiggles: ridge regularisation

Overfit polynomials usually have huge coefficients that cancel each other out. Ridge regression adds a penalty on coefficient size to the least-squares loss, so the model prefers smaller, smoother solutions:

$$\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 \;+\; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{d} \beta_j^2$$

\(\lambda = 0\) is ordinary least squares. As \(\lambda\) grows, the curve is pulled towards a flat line. The intercept \(\beta_0\) is not penalised. The data comes from the playground.

Coefficient sizes

Absolute coefficients on the scaled features (log scale): least squares vs ridge.

Ridge needs features on comparable scales, otherwise the penalty hits some coefficients far harder than others. Always standardise before regularising. Choose λ with cross-validation, exactly like the degree. Its sibling, Lasso (an L1 penalty), can shrink coefficients all the way to 0 and so selects features automatically.
