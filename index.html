1The model

Simple linear regression describes a target \(y\) as a straight-line function of one feature \(x\):

$$\hat{y} = m\,x + b$$
\(\hat{y}\) “y-hat”: the model’s prediction.
\(m\) slope: how much \(\hat y\) changes when \(x\) rises by one unit.
\(b\) intercept (bias): the prediction when \(x = 0\).
\(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) residual: the error on point \(i\). Positive means the line is too low.

Training means choosing \(m\) and \(b\) so that the residuals are as small as possible, overall. How we define “small overall” is the whole story of this lab.

3Why least squares?

A natural first idea is to add up the residuals. It fails, because positive and negative errors cancel: the flat line at the mean has a total residual of exactly zero, yet it ignores \(x\) completely.

Σ e (your line)
Σ |e| (your line)
Σ e² (your line)

Squaring every residual fixes the cancelling, punishes big misses more than small ones, and gives a smooth bowl-shaped loss with a single minimum. Setting its derivatives to zero gives the ordinary least squares (OLS) solution in closed form:

$$m = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}, \qquad b = \bar y - m\,\bar x$$

Turn on Squared errors in the playground: the best-fit line is the one that makes the total area of the squares as small as possible.

4Evaluation metrics

Once a model makes predictions, we need a single number that says how good they are. Each metric below answers a slightly different question. Live values use the best-fit line on the playground data.

Where the numbers come from

Every metric is built from the same residual table. MAE averages the |e| column, MSE averages the e² column, and RMSE takes its square root.

What R² really measures

R² compares your model with the simplest possible baseline: always predicting the mean \(\bar y\). SST is the baseline’s squared error, SSE is your model’s. R² is the share of the baseline error that the model removes.

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2}$$

Try dragging your line far away from the data: when SSE exceeds SST, R² goes negative, meaning the model is worse than just guessing the average.

Always true: MAE ≤ RMSE. The larger the gap, the more your error is concentrated in a few big misses.

5Outliers: MAE vs RMSE

One bad data point can dominate a squared-error metric. Move the red point up or down and compare how MAE and RMSE react.

MAE grows linearly with the outlier; RMSE grows much faster because the error is squared before averaging. If occasional big misses are acceptable, report MAE; if they are dangerous, report RMSE.

6Learning the line with gradient descent

OLS has a closed-form answer, but most machine-learning models do not. They are trained by gradient descent: start with a guess and repeatedly step downhill on the loss surface.

$$m \leftarrow m - \eta\,\frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial m}, \qquad b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial b}$$ $$\frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial m} = -\frac{2}{n}\sum_i x_i\,(y_i-\hat y_i), \qquad \frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial b} = -\frac{2}{n}\sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)$$

The data comes from the playground. Features are rescaled before training (as you would in practice); results are converted back to the original units.

Try η above ≈ 1 with z-scores, or above ≈ 0.6 with min–max scaling: the steps overshoot and the loss diverges. With un-centred features the bowl is a long narrow valley, so the same learning rate converges far more slowly. That is why we standardise features.

7Train / test & overfitting

Metrics computed on the data the model was trained on are optimistic. To estimate real-world performance we hold out a test set. Here we fit polynomials of increasing degree to curved data: training points and test points.

Degree 1 is a straight line: it underfits (high bias), with high error on both sets. Around degree 3–5 test error is lowest. Push to 10+ and training error keeps falling while test error explodes: the model has memorised the noise (overfitting, high variance). A negative test R² is a red flag.

8Is the slope real? Statistical output

Statistics packages report more than the fitted line. Each coefficient has a standard error (how much it would wobble across new samples), a t-statistic and a p-value for the null hypothesis “the true coefficient is 0”. This table updates with the playground data.

A tiny p-value says the relationship is unlikely to be pure chance. It does not say the model predicts well (check RMSE) or that x causes y.
